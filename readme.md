# [Modulation Identification] Uncertainty Increase Auto-modulation Classification Based on Bayesian Neural Networks [with MATLAB Code]

Introduction to Automatic Modulation Classification (AMC) is the process of identifying the modulation type based on its received signal at the receiver. Achievement of automatic configuration for receivers, reduction of system overhead, and enhancement of communication efficiency are of significant importance in both civilian and military applications. Traditional AMC can be broadly categorized into two types: 1) likelihood-based methods and 2) feature-based methods. In the likelihood-based approach, multiple candidate modulations are calculated by creating a likelihood function for each candidate modulation. Hypothesis testing is performed, and the modulation scheme with the highest likelihood value is selected [1]. Although this method has been proven to achieve optimal performance, its computational complexity is too high to be implemented in real-time systems. Moreover, in practical communication scenarios, it is difficult to obtain accurate likelihood functions. In the feature-based approach, if the features are well designed, performance can be achieved close to that of the likelihood-based method, with significantly reduced computational complexity. In literature, various features for AMC have been considered, including higher order moments and cumulants of the received signal [2], cyclic stability [3], and instantaneous amplitude, phase, and frequency [4]. However, these features require manual design based on expert knowledge about channel models and modulation schemes.
To address the shortcomings of traditional methods, data-driven approaches based on deep learning (DL) have recently garnered significant attention. With DL, it is not only possible to extract important features directly from raw data without human intervention but also achieve better classification performance than traditional feature-based methods [5]. In [6], well-known model architectures developed for image classification, such as the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) and Residual Network (ResNet), are also effective in AMC. In [7], [8] and [9], convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures have been designed for efficient AMC computation. In [10], [11], [12], [13] and [14], traditional recurrent neural network (RNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) have been adopted as effective model architectures for extracting time features from received signals in multipath fading channels and have shown superior accuracy compared to CNN. Moreover, LSTM can classify modulation schemes with any length of received signal sequences [11], [12], [13]. Comprehensive comparisons of DL-based AMC methods, including those mentioned earlier, have been provided in [15].
The aforementioned DL-based AMCs all rely on the frequency theory method, which aims to determine the optimal model parameters that minimize the expected loss function. Therefore, traditional DL-based AMCs produce point estimates of model parameters after training with datasets. Although this frequency theory method has been proven to achieve high classification accuracy in real-world scenarios, it does not provide reliable uncertainty information about its predictions. The output of the softmax activation function provides a rough estimate of prediction reliability, but it inherently has limitations in accurately representing actual prediction reliability due to its tendency to produce overconfident predictions [16]. For example, in challenging channel conditions with low Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and/or short received sequence lengths, the classifier may make incorrect predictions, while the corresponding softmax values often remain high. This overconfidence behavior of the frequency theory method becomes more apparent in real scenarios with small and/or noisy training datasets, making it challenging to directly use the softmax output to quantify prediction uncertainty. Moreover, when receiving sample sequences from new invisible modulation schemes, traditional frequency theory methods struggle to determine whether they match any candidate modulation. If prediction uncertainty information is available at the classifier, it can be further improved by processing predictions differently based on their uncertainty information to enhance communication efficiency [17]. Especially in safety-critical applications such as healthcare and autonomous driving, prediction uncertainty is as important as prediction accuracy to avoid dangerous situations.
To obtain reliable uncertainty information, we employ Bayesian methods in machine learning. Unlike frequency-based methods, Bayesian methods allow us to derive the model parameters and the probability distribution of predictions, thereby providing a more accurate assessment of prediction uncertainty. Despite its importance and potential, Bayesian learning has not been well studied in AMC, except for [18]. In [18], it was shown that Bayesian learning based on AMC provides good calibration of predictions with confidence scores closely matching actual accuracy. However, this work primarily focuses on the calibration performance of Bayesian methods, rather than the overall capabilities of the classifiers. The developed classifiers, based on the feedforward neural network (FNN) architecture, may have inherent limitations that make them unsuitable for solving some practical challenges. For example, they cannot handle variable-length sequences deviating from the training dataset and are difficult to effectively capture temporal features of received sequences in scenarios with limited training data.
The limitations of traditional AMC methods are addressed by proposing a new Bayesian incremental AMC method based on uncertainty perception. Specifically, our DL framework enables the classifier to not only provide reliable uncertainty estimates for its predictions but also maintain high prediction accuracy even when training on small datasets. Moreover, the purpose of the proposed method is to effectively retain these attributes while applying them to received sequences, compared to those used for training. This scalability provides new advanced AMC functionalities for Bayesian classifiers beyond the evaluation of prediction reliability. Firstly, the robust scalability of our method supports adaptive real-time AMC where the classifier progressively improves its prediction samples with additional signals as they are received, continuing until the confidence score meets the target classification accuracy, thereby minimizing the need for excess signal samples. Secondly, scalability also allows for identifying unseen modulation schemes that are missing during the training phase. The classifier can identify unseen modulation schemes by their unique confidence score patterns relative to the length of the received sequence. We validated the advantages of the proposed method over traditional learning-based AMC methods through several simulations on the RadioML 2018.01A dataset [6].
Article illustration
Conclusion In this article, we propose a novel Bayesian learning framework for incremental modulation classification, addressing the limitations of traditional DL-based AMC. Our method consists of two stages: 1) using a given training dataset to optimize model parameters through a cumulative loss function in a frequency-theoretic manner and 2) applying LA and VI among other BNN methods to the final layer of the model for statistical inference. Simulations on the RadioML 2018.01A dataset demonstrate that our framework outperforms traditional AMC methods in terms of classification accuracy and calibration performance. Notably, based on strong calibration performance, our classifier has been able to identify new, unseen modulation schemes. These findings not only underscore the effectiveness of our proposed framework but also pave the way for future explorations in AMC technology.
